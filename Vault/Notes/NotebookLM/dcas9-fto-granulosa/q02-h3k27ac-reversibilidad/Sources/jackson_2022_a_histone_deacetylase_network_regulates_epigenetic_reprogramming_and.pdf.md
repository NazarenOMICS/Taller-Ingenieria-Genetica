Vorinostat is rapidly cleared during clinical dosing(Ramalingam et al., 2007). We therefore also 149 tested if simulating in vivo pharmacokinetics via daily transient vorinostat exposure could prevent 150 HIV latency. Removal of vorinostat containing media for an 18 hour period was previously 151 demonstrated to facilitate clearance of the inhibitor from treated T cells(Clutton et al., 2016). 152 Infected CD4 T cells were exposed to either vehicle only, continuous vorinostat (500nM), or a 153 daily 6h pulse of vorinostat (500nM) followed by an 18h “wash-out” period (Figure 1G). Infected 154 cells were monitored for viral gene expression by flow cytometry for 21dpi (Figure 1H). As 155 observed above, continuous vorinostat potently prevented the emergence of latently infected cells 156 (3-fold increase in Thy1.2+/GFP+ cells). Pulsatile vorinostat also blocked latency but did not reach 157 the level of efficacy as continuous exposure (2.4-fold increase in Thy1.2+/GFP+ cells). 158  159 We also examined whether vorinostat could prevent re-establishment of HIV latency after viral 160 reactivation. We have previously reported that ex vivo-generated latently infected CD4 T cells can 161 be reactivated via their TCR to re-express HIV genes but then rapidly re-establish HIV silencing 162 after stimulus removal(Jefferys et al., 2021). We reactivated latently infected cells isolated 163 previously via TCR stimulation and, as expected, stimulated cells rapidly re-expressed GFP, 164 indicating latency reversal. After two days of activation, the TCR stimulus was removed, and cells 165 were exposed to vorinostat (500nM) or vehicle control (Figure S2A). Consistent with our previous 166 report, reactivated cells in the vehicle-treated group rapidly re-established latency, with viral gene 167 expression declining 2-fold by 7 days post-activation. By contrast, reactivated cells cultured with 168 vorinostat maintained active viral gene expression (GFP+). Thus, class I HDAC inhibition can 169 prevent both establishment and re-establishment of HIV latency after TCR activation. Overall, we 170 conclude that HDAC activity plays a crucial role in the early stages of latency establishment and 171 that class 1 HDACis block the development of latently infected CD4 T cells from a pool of 172 productively infected cells.  173  174 HIV-infected CD4 T cells maintain viral gene expression after HDACi withdrawal. 175 To further examine the impact of vorinostat on HIV gene expression, we exposed activated HIV-176 infected cells to a range of vorinostat concentrations for 14 days, followed by an additional 14-177 day culture in the absence of vorinostat. We observed a dose-dependent relationship between 178 vorinostat concentration and latency prevention, with a consistent trend toward latency reduction 179 observed even in the lowest dose of vorinostat (125nM) (Figure S2B). Based on the measurable 180 latency prevention observed at this low dose of vorinostat, we sought to compare the activity of 181 HDACis in latency prevention versus latency reversal. We tested HDACis for latency reversing 182 activity in two latently infected Jurkat cell lines - one with a provirus that is highly responsive to 183 LRAs (2D10 cells(Pearson et al., 2008), Figure S2C) and another that is relatively unresponsive 184 to LRAs (N6 cells(Nixon et al., 2020), Figure S2D). 2D10 cells were responsive to HDACis but 185 only at relatively high doses (vorinostat EC50: 671nM), while N6 cells were almost completely 186 unresponsive to the HDACis in the range tested.
